Who Owns the Method in a Data Science Team

A method prescribed from above costs a data science team the knowledge that sits close to the data, its commitment to the result and the early report of a failure, and a leader who sets the problem and its constraints gets work that the team owns.

Most people who have worked in an organisation have received an instruction that stated what the result should be and also how to obtain it. A leader who stood further from the work than the person who carried it out had written it. Such an instruction is usually well meant, since it saves a discussion and reflects a route that has worked before. Its effect on a capable team is nonetheless predictable. The team delivers what was specified and stops reporting what it sees, including the point at which the specified route fails. In a data science team the effect is stronger, because the route is the part of the work that is least known in advance. A brief that reads “build a random forest on these five features to predict churn by Friday” fixes the model, the inputs and the date before anyone has looked at the data.

In my experience of leading and working beside data science teams, this habit is a more persistent cause of disengagement than a shortage of technical skill or a weak infrastructure. The reason for the work, the outcome that it should achieve and the method that produces it are all settled at once, before the team has contributed. A team that may only carry out the prescription of someone else cannot do what it was hired to do, which is to find out what the data is able to say. Its members gradually cease to act as problem-solvers.

This post argues that the method should belong to the team, and that leadership should hold the reason and the outcome together with the constraints of the business. It sets out what a prescribed method costs. The second post turns the argument into a division of the work, states when a leader should specify the method after all, and describes three changes in practice. The argument rests on experience, on a small number of books on leadership and on three studies. It is not a measured result, and its limits are stated in the last section. The post also continues three earlier ones, on shared mental models, on group forces in organisational change and on the strategic kernel of an AI strategy. They supply the neighbouring arguments that are referred to below.

Leadership Tendency to Prescribe

A leader who can see a route to an answer is tempted to settle the whole of it: why the problem matters, which outcome to pursue and how to reach it. The tendency is natural, because the route looks clear from the position of the leader, and it is the habit that S. Eblin asks leaders to change. Each chapter of The Next Level begins with something to pick up and something to let go.1 Applied to a data team, the leader picks up the work of giving context, removing obstacles in the organisation and setting direction, and lets go of the choice of the model. The titles of Chapters 5 to 7, which form the part of the book on team presence, indicate the direction of the advice: team reliance, defining what to do, and accountability for many results.2 I read the three together as a division of labour. The leader relies on the team, defines what is to be done and holds the team accountable for the result, but does not carry out the work of the team in its place.

The chapter that gives this post its title, Chapter 6, adds an argument that rests on the development of the team. The discussion guide cited above asks leaders to recall the occasions on which they learned most, and observes that much of that development came when the what of the task was clear and the how was not, because the learning consisted of working out the how. A manager has the opportunity to create the same experience for the team, and is freed in doing so for the work that only the manager can do. The guide also asks which perspective on the what the leader holds that the team lacks, such as what is said in meetings and conversations that the team does not attend, and what the leader does to pass that perspective on. In the media kit of the book, Eblin writes that the way of the leader “may be the right way” but is “not the only right way”, and that the energy of the leader belongs in “defining what to do” and in giving the team “the space to figure out how”.3 This argument differs from the one in the next section, because it concerns what the team gains and not what the data requires. I read the two as coinciding in a data science team, since the person who works out the method also learns the data.

Eblin uses the image of the balcony and the dance floor in his coaching of leadership teams, where the aim is to get a team off the floor and onto the balcony.4 On the dance floor a leader sees the next step and the partner in front. From the balcony the leader sees the whole floor, including who is dancing with whom and where the floor is crowded or empty. Figure 1 draws the image. The walls of the floor are the constraints of the business, the paths of the dancers are the method, and only the balcony shows a corner that no dancer has used. A leader who stays on the floor chooses the next step for the team. A leader on the balcony asks what the floor requires and leaves the steps to the dancers. The image supplies a test for each method that appears in a brief. A method may be a constraint of the business, such as latency, privacy or budget. It then describes the floor and stays in the brief. Otherwise it is the step that the leader would have chosen personally, which makes it a preference that passes to the dancers.

T. Fadell draws a related line between decisions that rest on opinion and decisions that rest on data. He describes the first version of a product as led by the opinion of its maker and later versions as led by evidence.5 I read a data science project as closer to the later case, because the evidence lies in the hands of the team and not of the leader.

Cost of a Prescribed Method

The cost of a prescribed method has two sources that are specific to data science. The first is that a usable signal in the data is rarely known to exist before the exploration. A method chosen in advance is therefore a guess made by the person who is furthest from the data. The second is that the knowledge needed to choose well sits close to the data. F. A. Hayek made the general point in 1945. The knowledge on which a good decision depends, in his account, is knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place. It is held by the people who are present and not by those who command.6 The data scientists who work with the pipelines, the missing values and the anomalies hold that knowledge, and a manager in a meeting room holds a summary of it.

Figure 2 sets out what each side holds. Leadership holds the purpose of the work and the constraints of the business, and the team holds the pipelines, the anomalies and the signal in the data. The brief is the channel between them. Context and constraints pass to the team through the brief. The findings and the risks that the team sees need a channel in the opposite direction, which a leader who has already fixed the method has little reason to open.

Commitment and Ownership

A prescription also costs the team its commitment. P. Lencioni places commitment and accountability in the middle of the dysfunctions of a team. People commit to a decision when they have been heard on it, even if it is not the decision that they would have taken. A team that has not committed does not hold itself accountable for the result.7 A prescribed method removes the hearing. The team may deliver what was specified, but it has no reason to defend the specification or to report that the method does not work. The leader therefore learns of a failure late.

The same pattern appears in the account of organisational change in an earlier post on Lewin’s theory. Lewin’s method of action research asks a group to analyse the situation, to identify alternatives, and to choose and act. Change is then made with the people concerned and not to them.8 A brief that gives a data science team the problem and its constraints invites the team into the same cycle. A prescribed method has already completed the first two steps on the team’s behalf. A second earlier post, on shared mental models, describes the common understanding of goals, roles and tasks that distinguishes effective teams. It reports that teams in which leadership is shared show greater commitment and more sharing of information.9 A brief written as a problem builds that understanding of the goal in the open, because the team and its leaders discuss the aim and the constraints before the method is chosen.

Research on empowerment points the same way. A meta-analysis of studies of psychological empowerment, which includes the sense of autonomy and of influence over the work, links it to higher task performance and to higher job satisfaction.10 Figure 3 follows the chain from being heard to the result, in a form simplified from Lencioni. Under a prescribed method the team is not heard and the later links are not reached, whereas under a problem brief the chain runs to the result.

Limits of the Argument

The post rests on experience and on books written for leaders in general, not on a study of data science teams. The empirical sources show that empowerment is associated with performance and that shared leadership is associated with commitment, and they do not show that a prescribed method causes a team to disengage. The second post describes the division of the work that follows from the argument and the conditions under which it does not hold.
