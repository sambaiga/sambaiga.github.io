Value of a Forecast

An accuracy score says how far a forecast lies from what happened. It does not say what the forecast is worth to the person who acts on it. A distribution operator who must decide in the evening whether to warn a crew about tomorrow’s loading of a transformer gains from a forecast only through the decision, and the decision depends on what a warning costs and on what an unwarned overload costs.1 Two forecasts with the same error can therefore differ in value, and a forecast with a larger error can be worth more to one user than a more accurate one.

This series measures the value of two kinds of probabilistic forecast on one decision: a quantile forecaster, which returns a set of quantiles, and a parametric Laplace forecaster, which returns a location and a scale. The first part gives the definitions. It states the decision, defines the event that the decision is about, shows how each forecaster gives the probability of the event, and defines the two scores that follow, the Brier score for the quality of the probability and the relative economic value for its worth. The illustrations use simulated numbers and are labelled as such. The data and the trained forecasters come in Part 2.

Cost-Loss Decision

The simplest account of a warning is the cost-loss model.2 The user can protect against an event at a cost CC, whether or not the event happens. If the user does not protect and the event happens, the user suffers a loss LL with C<LC < L, and if the event does not happen there is no cost. A warning that precedes an event is a hit, a warning without an event is a false alarm, an event without a warning is a miss, and no warning without an event is a correct rejection, which costs nothing. The ratio of the two is the cost-loss ratio,

α=CL. \alpha = \frac{C}{L}.

A user who believes that the event has probability pp expects to lose pLpL by not protecting and CC by protecting. Figure 1 draws the two expenses against pp in units of LL: protecting costs the constant α\alpha, and not protecting costs pp. Protecting is cheaper when pL>CpL > C, which is the rule

p>α. p > \alpha.

The rule is the best one for a user whose probabilities are reliable, and it shows why the ratio belongs to the user and not to the forecast. A crew that can be redirected at little cost has a small ratio and warns on a weak signal, while an action that is expensive and disruptive has a large ratio and requires a strong one.

Events as Runs

The decision turns on an event, and the event has to be defined before it can be forecast or scored. For a transformer, the quantity is the apparent power SS in kVA at each half-hour. The thermal limit is set relative to the network before electric vehicles and photovoltaic generation were added, as a multiple cc of the 98th percentile of the apparent power S0S_0 of that network, and it is called the pre-DER thermal limit,

Slim=c⋅q0.98(S0). S^{\mathrm{lim}} = c \cdot q_{0.98}(S_0).

The limit measures what the network carried before distributed energy resources, and it is not the nameplate rating. It is chosen because it is exceeded more often as penetration grows, which keeps the event informative; an event defined by the rating can be too rare to score. The decision considered here concerns loading that persists, so the event requires that the apparent power stays above the limit for kk consecutive half-hours, with k=4k = 4 for two hours. For a day of HH half-hours the outcome is

o=maxtmin0≤j<k𝟙[St+j>Slim]. o = \max_{t} \, \min_{0 \le j < k} \, \mathbb{1}\left[S_{t+j} > S^{\mathrm{lim}}\right].

The outcome is one when some run of kk half-hours lies above the limit and zero otherwise. A value exactly at the limit is not above it, and a missing value ends a run.

Probability from Forecasts

A forecast gives the probability of the event when it gives a distribution for each half-hour. The two forecasters give it in different ways. A quantile forecaster returns the values q1<…<qKq_1 < \dots < q_K at levels τ1<…<τK\tau_1 < \dots < \tau_K and no distribution between or beyond them.[^koenker1978] Its distribution function is completed by linear interpolation between neighbouring levels,

F(y)=τk+y−qkqk+1−qk(τk+1−τk), F(y) = \tau_k + \frac{y - q_k}{q_{k+1} - q_k} \left(\tau_{k+1} - \tau_k\right),

and, for y>qKy > q_K, by an exponential tail beyond the outermost level, whose scale bKb_K is fixed by the two outermost quantiles on that side,

F(y)=1−(1−τK)e−(y−qK)/bK. F(y) = 1 - \left(1 - \tau_K\right) e^{-(y - q_K)/b_K}.

The tail matters because a limit usually lies beyond the outermost level, where the quantiles are silent and a linear extension would fix the probability arbitrarily. A Laplace forecaster returns a location μ\mu and a scale bb, and its distribution function is known in closed form. With the standardised distance z=(y−μ)/bz = (y - \mu)/b it is

F(y)=12+12sgn(z)(1−e−|z|). F(y) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} \, \mathrm{sgn}(z) \left(1 - e^{-\lvert z \rvert}\right).

For one half-hour, the probability that the apparent power exceeds the limit is one minus the distribution function at the limit,

p=1−F(Slim). p = 1 - F\left(S^{\mathrm{lim}}\right).

An event that lasts kk half-hours cannot be read from these marginal distributions, because it depends on how the half-hours move together. It is estimated instead from MM scenarios of the day, as the share of scenarios that contain the event,

p=1M∑m=1Mo(m). p = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} o^{(m)}.

Part 3 constructs the scenarios. The rest of this part uses events whose probability is given directly.

Figure 2 applies both forecasters to one half-hour of a simulated transformer whose apparent power has a right tail. The true probability of exceeding the limit of 62 kVA is 0.069. The quantile forecaster gives 0.071, with its nine quantiles drawn as hollow points and the exponential tail carrying the curve beyond the last one. The Laplace forecaster gives 0.057, which is 18% below the true value. The Laplace distribution is symmetric about its median, so a right tail that is heavier than a Laplace tail is understated, and an operator who warned at a ratio of 0.06 would warn under the quantile forecaster and not under the Laplace forecaster. The two forecasters can therefore disagree about the same day, and the scores below decide which disagreement costs more.

Scoring a Probability

The quality of a probability of an event is measured by the Brier score, the mean squared difference between the forecast probability pnp_n and the outcome on∈{0,1}o_n \in \{0, 1\} over NN days,3

BS=1N∑n=1N(pn−on)2. \mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} (p_n - o_n)^2.

The score is strictly proper, so the best expected score is reached only by stating the true probability.4 It separates into a reliability term, which measures the gap between the stated probabilities and the frequencies with which the event followed them, a resolution term, which measures how far the frequencies of the forecast classes lie from the overall rate, and an uncertainty term that depends only on the event rate.5 A reliable forecast has a small reliability term, and a forecast that separates the days with the event from the days without has a large resolution term. For a probability estimated from MM scenarios, the fair estimator removes the sampling term of a small ensemble.6 The skill compares the score with that of a reference that states a constant rate s‾\bar{s} taken from the training window,

BSS=1−BSBSref. \mathrm{BSS} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{BS}}{\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{ref}}}.

A skill of one is a perfect forecast, zero is no better than the reference, and a negative value is worse. A constant rate is a weak reference where the risk depends on the hour of the day, and the later parts also use the rate for each hour.

A simulated example shows what the three parts measure. Over 6,000 days the true probability of the event follows a Beta distribution with mean 0.25, and the outcome of each day is drawn from it. Two forecasts are scored on the last 3,000 days, with the rate of the first 3,000 as the reference. The calibrated forecast states the true probability. The over-confident forecast ranks the days in the same order but pushes its probabilities towards zero and one. The event rate on the test days is 0.247.

The calibrated forecast has a Brier score of 0.1678 and a skill of 0.098. Its reliability term is 0.0002, so its probabilities match the frequencies, and its resolution term of 0.0173 is the information that it adds to the event rate. The over-confident forecast has a Brier score of 0.1840 and a skill of 0.011. Its resolution of 0.0154 is similar, so it separates the days almost as well, but its reliability of 0.0154 is far larger than the 0.0002 of the calibrated forecast. The whole of its loss lies in the stated probabilities and none in the ranking of the days. A user who acts on these probabilities, as the next section shows, bears that loss. The reliability and the resolution are computed in ten equal probability bins.

Relative Economic Value

The value of a probability forecast to a user is the expense saved by acting on it, set against what the user would spend without it. Let a warning be issued when the probability exceeds α\alpha, and let aa be the days with a warning and an event, bb the days with a warning and no event, and cc the days with an event and no warning, among NN days. In units of LL the user who follows the forecast pays

Efc=α(a+b)+cN. E_{\mathrm{fc}} = \frac{\alpha (a + b) + c}{N}.

Without a forecast the user either always protects or never protects. With the event frequency ss, the better of the two pays

Eclim=min⁡(α,s), E_{\mathrm{clim}} = \min(\alpha, s),

and a user with perfect knowledge protects only on the days with an event and pays

Eperf=αs. E_{\mathrm{perf}} = \alpha s.

The relative economic value is the share of the possible saving that the forecast achieves,7

V(α)=Eclim−EfcEclim−Eperf. V(\alpha) = \frac{E_{\mathrm{clim}} - E_{\mathrm{fc}}}{E_{\mathrm{clim}} - E_{\mathrm{perf}}}.

It is one for a perfect forecast and zero for the better of always and never protecting, and it is negative where the forecast does worse than that. Consider 100 days with 20 events and a ratio of α=1/6\alpha = 1/6, so that a loss is six times the cost of acting. A forecast warns on 30 days, with 16 hits, 14 false alarms and 4 misses. Then Efc=(30/6+4)/100=0.090E_{\mathrm{fc}} = (30/6 + 4)/100 = 0.090, Eclim=1/6=0.167E_{\mathrm{clim}} = 1/6 = 0.167 and Eperf=0.033E_{\mathrm{perf}} = 0.033, so that V=0.077/0.133=0.575V = 0.077 / 0.133 = 0.575. The forecast achieves 58% of the saving that perfect knowledge would give this user.

Figure 3 gives the value of the two simulated forecasts for every ratio. The calibrated forecast has a value of at least 0.05 for ratios up to 0.47, with a maximum of 0.288 at a ratio of 0.25. The maximum lies near the event rate of 0.247. For ratios above 0.5 the value is close to zero. The over-confident forecast has a maximum of 0.224 at the same ratio, which is 22% lower, and it has a negative value for every ratio below 0.19, falling to −0.49-0.49 at a ratio of 0.10. A user for whom acting costs little would have done better by ignoring the forecast and following the better of always or never protecting.

The Brier score and the value therefore agree on the ranking here and disagree on how much it matters. The skill of the over-confident forecast, 0.011, says that it is about as good as the constant rate, and the value curve says for whom it is worse. The users who lose most are those with the smallest cost-loss ratios. A forecast is judged by the users that it is meant to serve, and the two forecasters of Part 2 are compared on that curve.

Limits of the Illustration

The numbers of this part are simulated. The probabilities, the outcomes and the apparent power of Figure 2 are drawn from stated distributions to show what the scores measure, and they are not results for a transformer. The value curve describes a family of users and not a single number, because the value of a forecast depends on the cost-loss ratio of the user, so Part 2 reports the whole curve and the ratios at which a forecaster is best. The skill is measured against a constant rate, which is a weak reference for a risk that depends on the hour of the day, and a skill against that reference can look larger than a skill against the rate for each hour. Finally, the days are treated as independent, whereas the probabilities of consecutive days are correlated, which widens the uncertainty of every score in this series beyond what the number of days suggests.

Next Steps

Part 3 constructs scenarios for the event that lasts two hours, shows how much a forecast that treats the half-hours as independent underestimates it, and measures what the dependence adds to the value.

Reproducible Results

The notebook computes every figure of this part from a seeded generator and the functions of twiga that score events and their value, twiga.core.metrics.events and twiga.core.metrics.decision. The event functions are in the main branch of twiga and are not yet in a release, so a release of twiga that contains them is needed to reproduce the notebook from the package index.
