Briefing a Data Science Team on the Problem and Not the Method

A leader who writes the brief as a problem divides the work with the team, keeps the constraints and the review points, and specifies the method only where maturity, regulation, safety or urgency require it.

The first post argued that a method prescribed from above costs a data science team three things: the knowledge that sits close to the data, its commitment to the result and the early report of a failure. The remedy is not to leave the team without direction. A brief that is written as a problem still states a great deal, and the leader keeps the choices that only the leader can make.

This post turns the argument into a division of the work. It sets out which of the three questions of a project belongs to leadership and which to the team, states the conditions under which the method should be specified after all, and describes three changes that a leader can make in practice. Like the first post, it rests on experience and on books on leadership, and its limits are stated in the last section.

Division of Responsibility

The first post leads to a division of the three questions that organise a project: why the work matters, which outcome it should move and how the outcome is reached. Leadership answers the first two together with product, and the data science team answers the third. The division works because each side holds what the other cannot see: leadership sees the purpose of the work and the limits of the business, and the team sees the data. A leader who states the aim does not need to choose the method, and a team that understands the aim is able to choose it. M. Cagan draws the same line between product teams and feature teams. The first is given a problem and measured on the outcome, and it chooses the solution. The second is given a roadmap and measured on output, and it builds a solution that was chosen for it.1

Two accounts of leading under pressure show the condition that makes the division safe. S. McChrystal describes a task force in Iraq that paired shared consciousness with empowered execution. Every unit understood the situation and the aim, and the people closest to the action decided how to act.2 L. D. Marquet, who commanded a nuclear submarine, summarises his practice as giving control to the crew while creating clarity about the aim.3 The condition in both accounts is clarity, since control without a shared aim leads to drift and clarity without control amounts to a command. A brief written as a problem supplies the clarity, and the method supplies the control. The motivation of the team points the same way. D. H. Pink lists technique, the way the work is done, among the four kinds of autonomy that sustain motivation, with task, time and team,4 and a brief that fixes the method removes one of the four.

Figure 1 shows the division for one project briefed in two ways. Under the prescriptive brief, leadership owns the reason, the outcome and the method. Under the problem brief, leadership and product own the reason and the outcome, for example a 5% reduction of the 30-day churn rate, and the data science team owns the method. The method is where a team that owns it can find that a model is not the right tool. A cohort analysis, a simple rule or a repair of the upstream logging may answer the question at a lower cost, and a team that holds only a specification has no occasion to report that.

The division also restates, for one project, the layers of the strategic kernel that the earlier posts on the kernel and on a five-step roadmapping process describe. The diagnosis, which those posts call Know-Why, and the guiding policy, which they call Know-What, are the work of leadership. The coherent actions, which they call Know-How, rest on the skills and the capabilities of the people who carry them out. The roadmap asks that every initiative connect back to the crux that the diagnosis identified. A brief written as a problem keeps that connection, because the team receives the crux and the constraints and chooses the action.

Conditions for Specifying the Method

Not every brief should be open. A. S. Grove ties the style of a manager to the task-relevant maturity of the person managed: a person who is new to a task needs structure and close direction, and a person who is experienced in it needs the objective and the freedom to choose the route.5 A data scientist in the first months, an incident that must be closed within hours, and a method fixed by regulation or by safety all call for specification. Figure 2 sketches the relation as a conceptual illustration and not as data. Direction falls as the maturity of the person for the task rises, regulation, safety or urgency raise it at any maturity, and the review points stay at every level.

Autonomy also has a cost where a team does not check itself. Langfred found, in self-managing teams, that high trust combined with high individual autonomy lowered the performance of the team when its members did not monitor one another’s work.6 The brief therefore fixes the constraints and the points of review, and the method stays with the team.

Changes in Leadership Practice

Three changes follow for a leader who recognises the habit, and Figure 3 summarises them. The first is to write the brief as a problem and not as a specification. In place of “build a predictive model for churn”, the brief states what is difficult to understand or to reduce, why it matters now, the constraints and the measure of success, and asks the team how it would investigate. The second is to put each method that appears in a brief to the test of the balcony described in the first post, so that only the constraints remain and the preferences pass to the team. The third is to give the team protected time and a regular forum in which data scientists present the problems that they found in the data. The team then originates work as well as receives it, and the share of capacity for that time is a local decision.

A leader who gives the team a hard problem and its constraints, and then trusts the team with the method, gains more than a better model. The team begins to act as the owner of the work and reports what it finds, whether or not it was asked.

Limits of the Argument

The division described here rests on the same experience and the same books as the first post, and it has not been measured. A leader can test the claim that ownership of the method improves the work in one team, by comparing a project briefed as a problem with one briefed as a specification.
